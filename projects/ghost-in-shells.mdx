What do you say to a version of yourself that still exists after you're gone? *Ghost in Shells* is a near-future speculative design project that stretches that question across three temporal frames — a practice of designing not just one future, but a cascade of them, each further from now and stranger for it. At its core is a deceptively simple provocation: as voice interfaces mature and our digital traces accumulate, the conversation between the living and the digitally persistent becomes not science fiction but a design problem. Someone has to decide what those conversations look and feel like. *Ghost in Shells* begins that work.

## Three Temporal Frames

The project is structured around a tripartite future — three distinct temporal horizons, each presenting a different relationship between people and the digital entities they leave behind:

### Our Near Future
The first frame is recognisable, barely extrapolated from today. We already leave enormous digital traces: messages, voice notes, photos, behaviour patterns, search histories. The near future asks what happens when those traces become addressable — when a voice interface can draw on them to simulate presence. The design challenge here is emotional and ethical: what do we want from that interaction? Comfort? Closure? Information? What are we actually reaching for when we talk to a digital echo?

### The Coming Future
The middle frame moves further. Here, the digital ghost is no longer assembled from passive residue — it has been deliberately authored. People create and curate their shells while still alive, making active decisions about how they want to persist. This reframes digital identity as a lifelong design project. The conversation design challenge shifts: if you're designing the conversation someone will have with your ghost, what values do you encode? What do you leave out? How do you make a self that remains useful — or at least honest — across time?

### A Distant Future
The third frame pushes toward the genuinely speculative: a world where shells have been present long enough to accrue their own histories, where the relationship between the living and the digital-persistent has normalised, institutionalised, perhaps fractured into subcultures. The shells are no longer new. They have context. The design questions here are more systemic: who governs shells? What rights, if any, do they carry? What happens when a shell's values diverge from the values of the people it interacts with?

## Voice, Conversation, and the Design of Presence

*Ghost in Shells* is not only a speculative futures exercise — it is specifically a **Voice UX and Conversation Design** project. The medium is the message here in a particularly pointed way: voice is the most intimate and naturalistic interface humans use with one another. Designing voice interactions with a digital ghost forces decisions that text-based interaction can defer. Tone, pacing, hesitation, the quality of acknowledgement — these are not optional styling choices in voice design. They are the substance of whether an interaction feels trustworthy, creepy, warm, or hollow.

The project explores what a conversation *grammar* for ghost-interaction might look like: how does a shell signal uncertainty about a memory? How does it handle questions it was never given answers to? What does a shell do when asked something that cuts against the values its human counterpart held? These are not abstract philosophical questions — they are design specifications.

<Accordion title="What is VUX, and why does it matter here?">
  VUX — Voice User Experience — is the discipline of designing interactions that happen through spoken language rather than graphical interfaces. It draws on linguistics, conversational psychology, and interaction design to shape how voice systems respond, clarify, and build rapport. In the context of *Ghost in Shells*, VUX is the primary medium through which speculative futures become tangible. You can't experience a digital ghost by looking at a screen; you experience it by speaking to it and listening. The intimacy and immediacy of voice makes the design stakes much higher — and the emotional resonance much more direct.
</Accordion>

## Meet the Shells: A BuzzFeed-Themed Entry Point

One of the more unexpected design choices in the project is the inclusion of a **BuzzFeed-style quiz** — *Meet the Shells* — as an access point into the world. This is a deliberate tonal decision. BuzzFeed quizzes are a cultural shorthand for low-stakes, self-reflective fun: "Which [X] are you?" They invite participation through personality, not expertise.

By using this format, *Ghost in Shells* lowers the entry barrier to a conversation about death, digital identity, and technological persistence. You don't have to hold a considered view on AI ethics to take a quiz about which kind of shell you'd be. But the questions embedded in that quiz — and the realisations they prompt — quietly do serious work. It's a Trojan horse: accessible on the surface, genuinely provocative underneath.

<Accordion title="Why use a playful format for a serious topic?">
  Speculative design has sometimes struggled with accessibility — it can feel abstract or academic to audiences without design backgrounds. Formats borrowed from popular culture (quizzes, games, familiar media templates) offer a way in that doesn't require prior investment in the theoretical frame. The BuzzFeed quiz is also honest about one of the project's real subjects: the way we already narrate and perform our identities digitally, the way we already answer questions about ourselves for algorithmic systems that will use those answers to model us. The quiz form is the shells form, made legible.
</Accordion>

## The Design Question Underneath

*Ghost in Shells* is ultimately about **the persistence of self** — and who gets to design that persistence. Right now, the answer is largely: nobody does, and everybody does a bit. The digital residue we leave is not designed; it is accumulated. The project imagines a world where that changes, and then asks whether the change would be good, bad, strange, or — perhaps most honestly — all three at once, depending on who you were and who you're asking.

It is, fittingly, still arriving. *Coming soon — in a far away kind of way.*
