What if taking a painkiller required the same deliberate attention as prayer? *Absolve* begins with a disarmingly ordinary observation — a family member who would not leave the house without their keys, phone, wallet, and Solpadeine — and transforms it into a philosophical artefact about how unremarkably we've absorbed pharmaceutical dependency into daily life. This is speculative design at its sharpest: not a product to be sold, but a provocation designed to be felt.

## The Provocation

Modern life has quietly reframed pain. Rather than treating it as a signal — the body's alarm system — we treat pain itself as the illness. The headache is the problem, not whatever the headache might be telling us. And the solution is instant: a pill pop away, no different in its logic from tapping a notification or refreshing a feed. Instant gratification has colonised medicine.

What makes this stranger still is brand loyalty. People don't just take painkillers — they take *their* painkillers. Panadol versus Nurofen versus the pharmacist's own generic. The same active molecules, vastly different emotional relationships. Marketing has layered performance, identity, and trust onto ibuprofen. Non-prescription drugs have become consumer products, governed by the same psychology as a coffee order or a trainer brand.

> *"I just love the fizz of it, I know the good feelings aren't far away."*

That quote — unsettling in its warmth, its intimacy with a pharmaceutical — is the emotional core of *Absolve*. The speaker isn't describing addiction in any clinical sense. They're describing affection. A comfort ritual. That's the territory this piece occupies.

## Design Approach

The project reframes the pharmacy as a **church for pain relief** and Solpadeine as **daily bread** — drawing on the structural logic of religious ritual to expose the devotional character already present in how people relate to over-the-counter medicine. This isn't satire for its own sake; it's a translation into a register that makes the invisible visible.

The aesthetic language is deliberate and layered:

- **Laboratory aesthetics** — clinical surfaces, precise instrumentation — act as a constant reminder that these are manufactured chemical compounds, synthesised in controlled environments by scientists, not organic remedies grown in nature. The intimacy we feel toward them is constructed.
- **A sensor pressure pad** — the user must press down with increasing force before the tablet is dispensed. The physical build of pressure mirrors the build of pain that ostensibly justifies the tablet. Applying effort before receiving relief reintroduces the friction the modern painkiller market has worked hard to eliminate.
- **Tablet splitting and dissolution** — the user offers half of their tablet back, a tithe to the 'Pain Killing Gods', an act of gratitude or perhaps bargaining. The dissolving half makes the chemical process visible: the tablet disappearing into water as it would into a bloodstream, the substance becoming invisible but active.

Taken together, these elements constitute a **designed ritual** — a conscious, value-laden construct, not merely a habit. The piece asks: if we slowed down and made painkiller intake ceremonial, would we still take them as readily?

## The Critical Challenge

*Absolve* sits squarely in the tradition of critical and speculative design: it doesn't solve a problem, it sharpens one. The danger it identifies is not that painkillers exist or that people use them — it's the **blasé quality of that use**. The passivity. The affection without awareness.

By adding friction through fiction, the piece forces a small hesitation — the kind of pause that ritual demands — into a transaction that typically happens on autopilot. The pharmacy-as-church metaphor doesn't mock religious practice; it borrows its seriousness and applies it to a context we've agreed to treat as trivial. In doing so, it asks whether that agreement was wise.

<Accordion title="Why ritual as the entry point?">
  The project began not with painkillers but with the concept of ritual itself — understood as an automation process, but a *conscious* one. Habits are automatic; rituals are intentional. They encode values, mark transitions, and create meaning through repetition. The design question became: what happens when we apply ritual's deliberateness to something we've made entirely automatic? The painkiller scenario emerged as a near-perfect test case — ubiquitous, emotionally loaded, commercially saturated, and almost entirely unreflective in practice.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What does 'friction through fiction' mean in practice?">
  Speculative design often works by making the familiar strange — defamiliarisation as a design method. The 'fiction' here is the church framing, the offering, the gods. None of it is literally true. But the friction it generates — the pause, the gesture, the divided tablet — is physically real. The user performs the ritual even knowing it is constructed. That performance is the point: if the ceremony changes how the act feels, what does that say about how ceremony shapes all the acts we think are purely practical?
</Accordion>

## Key Elements at a Glance

| Element | Function |
|---|---|
| Pharmacy-as-church framing | Reveals the devotional logic already present in brand loyalty and medicine ritual |
| Pressure sensor pad | Reintroduces physical friction before tablet delivery; mirrors pain accumulation |
| Tablet splitting & offering | Creates a gesture of gratitude/acknowledgement toward the substance's power |
| Dissolution visual | Makes the chemical act of absorption legible — invisible process, made visible |
| Laboratory aesthetic | Grounds the piece in the manufactured, scientific reality of pharmaceutical production |
