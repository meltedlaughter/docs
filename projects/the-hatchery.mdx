Knowledge doesn't flow through organisations like water through pipes — it moves through people. The way it spreads, transforms, and becomes useful depends almost entirely on the relationships and moments of encounter that either happen or don't happen in the spaces where we work. The Hatchery is a workplace concept designed around that insight: a system of spaces and objects engineered to make knowledge-sharing not just possible, but inevitable.

<Info>
  **Year:** 2014 · **Award:** RSA Student Design Award Winner · **Disciplines:** Architecture / Industrial Design / Interior Design / Systems Design
</Info>

## The Challenge

Most workplaces are designed for individual productivity. Desks, meeting rooms, corridors — the spatial grammar of the typical office is built around containment and separation. Knowledge moves between people in spite of this layout, through chance encounters and informal conversations that happen in the margins of the designed space, not because of it.

The Hatchery posed a direct challenge to this model. If we accept that the people within an organisation are its most valuable nodes of knowledge — each person a connector and translator of information that no-one else holds in quite the same way — then the workplace should be designed to activate those nodes, not suppress them.

## The Design Concept

The concept positions people as nodes in a knowledge network, where the value of an individual lies not just in what they know, but in their capacity to connect information between others. In any organisation, certain people act as translators — they understand multiple domains, speak multiple professional languages, and can take an idea from one context and make it legible in another. The Hatchery was designed to find those people, support them, and create the conditions for their knowledge to move.

Objects and spaces within The Hatchery are conceived as connecting nodes — points of high engagement where people naturally pause, interact, and exchange. Rather than designing for the meeting (a scheduled, intentional act), the system is designed for the encounter: the unplanned moment of contact that so often carries the most productive energy.

## Design Approach

<Steps>
  <Step title="Research & Systems Mapping">
    Mapped the informal knowledge flows within organisations — identifying where ideas moved freely, where they stalled, and what kinds of spatial and social conditions enabled or blocked exchange.
  </Step>
  <Step title="People as Nodes">
    Developed the conceptual framework of individuals as connectors and translators of knowledge — moving beyond the idea of expertise as a fixed attribute to knowledge as something that lives in relationships between people.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Spatial Strategy">
    Defined a spatial typology for The Hatchery — identifying the kinds of spaces needed to support different modes of knowledge exchange, from the quiet, focused encounter to the large, energised collision.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Object Design">
    Designed the objects that would populate the space — each one conceived as a social connector, something that gives people a reason to pause and engage rather than pass through.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Systems Integration">
    Brought the spatial and object strategies together into a coherent system — ensuring that The Hatchery functioned as a whole, not just as a collection of interesting individual pieces.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Key Design Principles

### Objects as Connectors

In The Hatchery, objects are not furnishings — they are catalysts. Each designed object is placed and configured to create a moment of engagement: a reason to stop, to look, to interact with another person. The design draws on research into the conditions under which people make spontaneous contact, and uses spatial and material cues to engineer more of those moments.

### Hierarchy of Encounter

The spatial system recognises that different kinds of knowledge exchange require different conditions. Some insights emerge in one-to-one conversation; others need the energy of a group. The Hatchery provides a hierarchy of encounter spaces — from intimate pockets for focused exchange to open areas where larger collisions can occur — and designs the transitions between them as carefully as the spaces themselves.

### Amplifying the Translator

At the heart of the concept is the recognition that some people are disproportionately valuable as knowledge connectors. The workplace should make those people visible and accessible — not by identifying them formally, but by creating conditions in which their natural tendency to connect and translate is amplified by the environment around them.

## Recognition

The Hatchery was awarded the RSA Student Design Award — recognition from one of the UK's most prestigious design organisations that the concept represented a genuine contribution to thinking about the future of workplaces and knowledge organisations.

## Outcome

The Hatchery remains a compelling proposition for any organisation grappling with the challenge of making distributed knowledge move. It demonstrates that architecture and industrial design, when approached through a systems lens, can fundamentally change the social dynamics of a workplace — not through surveillance or management intervention, but through the quiet intelligence of well-designed space.
