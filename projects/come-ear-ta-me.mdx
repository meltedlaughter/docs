*Come ear ta me* — Dublin slang, a warm compression of "Come here to me," which means something between *listen up*, *pay attention*, and *let me tell you something*. It's an invitation. It implies proximity, shared space, a moment of contact between people who might otherwise pass without acknowledgment. As a title for a Masters Interaction Design project rooted in playful urban experience, it couldn't be more precise. The project asks: what does it mean to design for the city not as infrastructure, but as invitation?

## The Playful City

The concept of the **Playful City** sits at the intersection of game design, urban planning, and social interaction — it proposes that cities are not only functional systems to navigate but stages for human connection, spontaneity, and delight. Most urban design optimises for flow: getting people from A to B efficiently. *Come Ear Ta Me* asks what happens when you optimise instead for encounter, for the unexpected social moment, for the kind of interaction that can only happen in a shared physical space.

This is not frivolity for its own sake. Play is a serious mode of human engagement. It lowers social barriers, invites participation from people who might not respond to more formal structures, and creates the conditions for genuine exchange. A city that makes room for play is a city that makes room for its people — not just as commuters or consumers, but as social beings with curiosity, spontaneity, and the need to connect.

<Accordion title="What is the 'Playful City' concept in design terms?">
  The Playful City is a framework in urban and interaction design that draws on the psychology of play — intrinsic motivation, voluntary participation, bounded rules, and meaningful feedback — and applies it to public space. Rather than designing for passive use (benches to sit on, paths to walk), the playful city designs for active engagement: surfaces that respond, objects that invite curiosity, situations that create opportunities for strangers to interact. Projects in this space borrow from game design, social psychology, behavioural economics, and service design to create urban experiences that are felt as fun but function as social infrastructure.
</Accordion>

## Masters Interaction Design: A Year's Work

*Come Ear Ta Me* is the culmination of a year-long Masters in Interaction Design — a context that shaped both the ambition and the methodology of the project. The Masters frame demands a particular kind of rigour: not just making something that works, but being able to articulate why it works, what it's in conversation with intellectually, and what its broader implications are.

The project sits within a tradition of thinking about design as a force that creates culture, not just products. As Wilson Miner wrote (and as Kevin's own writing engages directly): *"When we make things, we create an orbit for other things."* The city is already a designed system — *Come Ear Ta Me* asks how a new, playful layer of interaction might disturb that system in generative ways.

The year of Masters study behind the project is visible in its scope. This is not a single artefact but a **service design proposition** — an ecosystem of touchpoints, interactions, and designed moments that together constitute an experience larger than any single element.

## How It Works

The project operates through a series of **designed encounters in public urban space** — interventions that invite participation without demanding it, that reward curiosity, and that create conditions for interaction between strangers or a deeper relationship between a person and their city.

Rather than prescribing behaviour, the interactions are structured to be:

- **Optional and voluntary** — they work through invitation, not obligation. Nobody is required to engage; the design makes engagement attractive.
- **Physically grounded** — they exist in the real city, in specific places, through objects and prompts that are part of the urban environment rather than overlaid digitally on top of it.
- **Socially generative** — the interactions are designed to create the conditions for connection, not to replace it. The technology or object is the catalyst; the human moment is the outcome.
- **Playfully framed** — the visual language, the names, the mechanics all carry a lightness that signals: *this is allowed to be fun*.

<Accordion title="How does service design fit into an urban interaction project?">
  Service design looks at an experience as a system — considering every touchpoint a person has with something over time, and designing them to work together coherently. Applied to urban interaction, this means thinking beyond the single object or intervention to consider: How does someone first encounter this? What happens if they engage? What happens if they don't? What changes over time? What does the experience feel like at the beginning, middle, and end? Service design tools — journey mapping, service blueprints, touchpoint analysis — bring this systemic thinking to what might otherwise be conceived as a series of one-off moments, giving the project coherence and depth.
</Accordion>

## The Interaction Model

At the heart of the project is a recognition that **interaction in the city is already designed — mostly for transactions and efficiency**. The bus stop, the ATM, the pedestrian crossing: these are all designed interactions in public space, but they are designed to minimise time and friction, to get the human task done and move on.

*Come Ear Ta Me* proposes a complementary layer: interactions designed to *maximise* a certain kind of friction — not frustration, but the productive delay of curiosity, the slight pause of encountering something unexpected and deciding whether to engage. These moments of designed hesitation are where the social potential lives.

The title's Dublin warmth is not incidental. The project is deeply place-specific — it is interested in the *texture* of a city, in the particular character of social interaction in an Irish urban context, in the kind of casual, warm address that Dublin slang carries. "Come ear ta me" is not a formal invitation; it's a neighbourly one. The project aims for that register: approachable, grounded, local.
