The home is one of the most constrained and most contested spaces in modern life. For children, it is often a place defined by adult priorities — furniture arranged for adults, routines structured around adult schedules, spaces designed for adult aesthetics. Play Pods asks what happens when you design the home differently: not just as a place to live in, but as a place to play in — and in doing so, to connect the people who live nearby.

<Info>
  **Year:** 2018 · **Disciplines:** UX / Social Design / Interaction Design / Service Design
</Info>

## The Challenge

Play is not a luxury for children — it is developmental infrastructure. But access to quality play is increasingly uneven, shaped by the size of a family's home, the safety of their street, and the availability of outdoor space. At the same time, communities are becoming more fragmented: neighbours who share walls often don't share anything else.

Play Pods was designed to address both problems simultaneously. By creating adaptable play spaces that open up the home — making it porous to the neighbourhood rather than sealed off from it — the project proposes a model of domestic space that serves children and builds community at the same time.

## The Design Approach

The concept centres on the idea that play doesn't require a dedicated room or an expensive installation. It requires a space that *permits* play — physically, socially, and in terms of the norms that govern how a home is used. Play Pods are modular, adaptable interventions that can be introduced into existing homes without major structural change, but which signal clearly that this is a place where children are welcome to be children.

The service design layer is equally important. Play Pods are not purely a product — they operate within a network of neighbours, facilitated by a platform that allows families to coordinate, share access, and build the relationships that turn individual home interventions into a genuine neighbourhood resource.

## Key Design Dimensions

### The Physical Space

Play Pods were designed to work within the constraints of typical domestic architecture — taking into account the tension between adult living space and children's play needs, and finding configurations that serve both without requiring one to entirely give way to the other.

The pods themselves are conceived as adaptable: configurable to different ages, different types of play, and different spatial constraints. The emphasis is on robustness, sensory richness, and the kind of open-endedness that allows children to direct their own play rather than following prescribed activities.

### The Social Layer

A key insight driving the project is that physical spaces alone don't create community — they create the *possibility* of community. The service design component of Play Pods addresses how neighbours discover, access, and build relationships around shared play infrastructure.

This included thinking through the platform and communication tools that would allow families to coordinate informally, the norms and trust signals that would make parents comfortable opening their homes, and the ways the network might grow organically from a single installation to a street-level resource.

### Interaction Design

The interaction design work focused on the moments of transition — how children move between home and pod, how parents coordinate and communicate, how the space signals its availability and its boundaries. These micro-interactions, both digital and physical, are what make the difference between a space that feels inviting and one that feels ambiguous or inaccessible.

## Outcome

Play Pods is a provocation as much as a proposal. It challenges the assumption that children's play is a private matter, contained within individual homes and families, and imagines instead a model of domestic space that is genuinely shared — where opening your home to play becomes a way of opening it to community. The project demonstrates the potential of service and interaction design to address social fragmentation through thoughtful, human-centred spatial interventions.
