> "We shape our tools and our tools shape us."

That observation — simple on the surface, vast underneath — sits at the heart of how I think about design. As more of the tools we live with every day become digital rather than physical, our opportunity and responsibility as designers is multiplying. We live in a world of screens, and we are the ones who decide what goes on them.

<Info>
  **Wilson Miner, *When We Build*:**
  "We are in a unique position to have an impact — one that lasts longer than the next redesign or the latest technology. What happens when we stop thinking of ourselves not just as developers or experience designers, and take up the mantle as a new generation of product designers for a digital world?"
</Info>

Those words stayed with me long after I first encountered them — not least because they echoed so closely the ideas Paul Adams raised at a recent AGM of the IDI. Both Miner and Adams referenced media theorist Marshall McLuhan, and both circled the same territory: the deep, often invisible connections between what we build and the environment those things create around them.

Think of the infrastructure of roads. The automobile didn't just change how people moved — it changed what time and distance *meant*. The technology and its infrastructure rewrote the social fabric. What we make carries that same potential.

---

## When We Build

When we make things — objects or systems — we create an orbit for other things. In such a connected world, what we make has varying levels of gravitational pull, creating different levels of disturbance on the wider system.

This is not a marginal concern. It is the central question of design practice. Every interface, every product, every system we put into the world exerts a force on the people who use it and on the broader ecosystem around them. The question is never simply *does it work?* — it is *what does it set in motion?*

Design has always been about connecting. Connecting the user to their goal. Information to its display. Objects to a place. In the past, an industrial designer made physical things to act as conduits — the touch points for interaction. But that touchpoint has become deeper, expanding into the metaphysical flesh beneath the skin of our objects.

When you aim to make more than a static object — when you make an experience or an engagement — you can change a behaviour or make a habit. You can alter how people do things. You create a culture, and from it, project societies.

---

## Designing for Connection

In a past RSA Student Design project, a classmate and I developed an insight that has shaped my thinking ever since: people are nodes of knowledge. It is the individual acting as both connector and translator of information. What we needed to amplify and channel was that capacity — through the greater system, through a working flow method on which we build spaces and objects to help infer this direction. Objects once static can become places of high engagement and connection.

This is what drew me to hackathons — not to compete, but to observe makers. To understand how people from various disciplines create things starting from baselines. These spaces represent the beginning of the next technological shift. Designing from the ground up. Learning by doing.

Bret Victor captures this expansively in his vision for the future of maker spaces, broken into three progressive stages:

1. **Seeing inside** — understanding the space through sensors, developing literacy for what is actually happening beneath the surface.
2. **Seeing across time** — using collected data to see patterns, to recognise what has happened and anticipate what might.
3. **Seeing across possibilities** — considering the connection of uses to predict applications, mapping the ripple effects of what we make before we make them.

Each stage is a deeper form of understanding. Each requires designers who are not just skilled, but genuinely curious about how systems behave.

## Legibility and Transparency

There is a seductive idea in design culture that the best interface is an invisible one — that seamlessness is the ultimate goal. I've grown wary of it.

<Note>
  **Timo Arnall:**
  "Intentionally hiding the phenomena and materiality of interfaces, smoothing over the natural edges, seams and transitions that constitute all technical systems, entails a loss of understanding and agency for both designers and users of computing. Lack of understanding leads to uncertainty and folk-theories that hinder our ability to use technical systems, and clouds the critique of technological developments."
</Note>

When we over-smooth, we obscure. We strip away the feedback loops that let people understand — and therefore trust and critique — the systems they inhabit. This matters more now than it ever has. Fake news, deep fakes, algorithmically curated realities: these are not fringe concerns. They are the wicked problems that emerge when people cannot read the systems acting upon them.

Anthony Dunne's framing of machine legibility has always resonated with me deeply:

<Info>
  **Anthony Dunne:**
  "A machine is beautiful when it's legible, when its form describes how it works. It isn't simply a matter of covering the technical components with an outer skin, but finding the correct balance between the architecture of the machine… and an expressive approach that is born out of the idea of interaction with those using the object."
</Info>

Dunne explored in his NCAD graduate thesis how form and function disconnected with mass production — how housing cases led us to lose honesty and transparency with our objects. But we now live in a time when we can make two-way connections again. When the digital and the physical can speak to each other, when an object can show you something of its inner life.

The digital shadows of our content and objects are not a nuisance — they are a form of communication. If we don't allow our future objects to cast those shadows, we will be left in a world of darkness.

---

## The Future Consumer

The current generation — and the future consumer even more so — will be more technologically literate than the majority of designers working today. They will actively seek to understand and hack their consumer products. They will expect depth, not just polish.

We must design with that in mind. We need to move back from the notion of the seamless experience as an absolute ideal, because at its extreme it costs us our understanding of how the grander system operates. Legibility is not the enemy of beauty. Transparency is not the enemy of elegance. They are, in fact, what makes both meaningful.

The responsibility that comes with designing for a connected, literate world is not a burden — it is the most interesting design brief there is.
